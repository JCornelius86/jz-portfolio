## Overview

We have three young kids who are picky eaters, and a standing goal of not serving chicken
nuggets every night. So every Sunday morning my wife and I sit down and plan the week: what
the family is eating, all seven days.

That part is fine. It is the next part that is miserable. You take those seven recipes,
cross-reference every ingredient, consolidate the duplicates, remember you are already out
of garlic, and then retype the entire thing into a store app that was not designed by
anyone who enjoys using it. It is an hour of pure translation, every single week, forever.

So MealMapper follows one straight line and removes the friction at every step:

> plan the week → auto-build the grocery list → order it, without retyping a thing.

Planning only works if we are both looking at the same week, so it is built around a
household rather than a user. Both of us edit the same plan and the same list from our own
phones and see each other's changes live.

There are two versions of this app in these shots. The first got the loop working. The second
is what came back after other people used it.

## What it does

The week is the home screen. Tap a day and pick from your meal library, filtered by how it
cooks (Quick Prep, Slow Cooker, Rice Cooker), or hit **Surprise Me**. Paste a recipe URL and
MealMapper pulls in the photo, ingredients, steps, and times. From the planned week it builds
one grocery list, merging duplicate ingredients and sorting them in the order you walk a store.
Then it matches each item to a real Kroger product with live prices and sends the whole cart
over in one pass, or hands the list to Instacart.

## Where it stalled

**The store integration is the whole product, and only one store cooperated.** Getting the
Kroger connection working was the moment the thing felt real. Then I went looking for the next
store and found that most of them have no usable public API at all. Instacart has effectively
cornered that ground. We order from Harris Teeter, whose own app is unfriendly enough to have
started this in the first place, so I knew exactly what limited reach would mean.

**Then life did what life does.** Work hit a busy patch, three young kids plus a demanding job
does not leave much for a fifth project, and an afternoon looking at how crowded meal planning
is on the App Store did not help. So it sat for six months.

## What handing it to other people changed

What got me back into it was putting the app in front of friends and family and watching what
happened. Version one had been reviewed by exactly two people who already knew how it worked.
Everything I had built was legible to me, so none of it looked broken to me.

The list that came back was not about meal planning at all. People got stuck at the door:
password reset was reachable but not actually completable, and there was no Google sign-in, so
the first thing the app asked of a new person was to invent another password. The tab bar icons
were decoration rather than signage. And nobody arriving at an empty week had any idea what to
do with it.

Underneath that was a structural one. I had organized meals into **collections**: folder cards
you tapped into, like a file system. Everyone I watched used them once, during setup, and then
never opened them again. Nobody wants a second job filing their dinners.

## The second pass

Version two fixed what testing surfaced and rebuilt the look. The design system, Deep Teal &
Mango, defaults to dark with hard outlines and offset shadows, and runs a light theme off the
same tokens. Google sign-in, a password reset that actually completes, a monochrome icon set in
place of the emoji, and a four-slide tour on first run that shows the loop before handing anyone
an empty week.

Then there is the class of thing only real use finds. Recipe steps could only be appended, so
fixing one typed out of order meant retyping every row after the mistake. They drag to reorder
now. And in the Kroger matcher, the last unmatched item could not be skipped: press Skip and the
same item came back forever. Every one of those is invisible in a demo and infuriating on a
Sunday.

## Everyone who eats here

The shared account started as two logins on one plan. It got more useful once I started
treating the household as a list of people. A member is anyone who eats at the table, which is
not the same as anyone who can sign in. Our four-year-old is a member. She is never getting a
password. So members carry an age range and dietary notes, and separately an optional login.

That made the obvious next feature possible. Any member can react to a meal: 😋 liked, 😐 fine,
🙅 nope. Every row is tappable, not just yours, because somebody has to record the toddler's
opinion, and tapping an active reaction clears it, because the phone is getting passed around
a dinner table. When every current member likes a meal, it joins the **Clean Plate Club**.
That is recomputed from the current member list every time, so adding a kid re-derives the club
instead of leaving a stale trophy on a meal half the house dislikes.
